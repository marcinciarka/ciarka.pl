Jak się tu mieszka?

Pick any spot in Warsaw or Kraków and get a plain-language report on what living there is like. The name is Polish for "What is it like to live here?".

In short

Jak się tu mieszka? answers one question about any spot in Warsaw or Kraków: what is it like to live here? The facts exist, but they are spread over a dozen public portals, and nobody reads them all for one address. The site keeps its own copies of that public data, so a typical report needs no outside request and takes under a second. Walking times follow the street network, and every score is simple arithmetic. I designed it and run it alone, and I used AI coding agents for much of the implementation. The first version took 8 days.

Chapter 1

A report answers one question about one address

You type an address or click the map. The site builds a report on what living there is like, and ends with a verdict in plain words.

The report is written for someone weighing up an address. It lists recent flat sale prices nearby, things worth checking in person, and how well the place suits families, seniors or people without a car.

The report has four chapters: getting around, everyday life, environment and the area. Each topic opens with one sentence, such as "City centre in 13 min.", then a score out of 100, charts and a map. It also covers facts that are hard to judge on a viewing. These include road noise at the building, flood hazard, and recent flat sale prices nearby. It also gives the hours of direct sun on 21 December, worked out from 3D models of the buildings around.

The verdict at the end lists pros, cons and things worth checking in person. From there a visitor can compare up to four places side by side, or open a heat map of the whole city to see where to look. There are no accounts. Saved places and preferences stay in the visitor's own browser.

The name stays Polish in the English version too. It is the question a person asks before they move.

What is in a report

Sections: transit, amenities, noise, air, environment with flood, local city facts, surroundings and step-free access. Each section runs with a 15 s timeout, and a report shows what it has if one section fails. A point within 50 m of a stored report reopens that report instead of building a new one. Reports are stored once, in English, and translated when they are sent, so every report reads in both languages. The Polish phrasebook is typed against the English one (Catalogue<typeof en>), so a missing Polish phrase fails the type check. Sunlight is computed for 21 December, 20 March and 21 June, at ground level and at about the 3rd floor, from GUGiK LoD1 building heights (153,109 buildings in Warsaw). Its limits are on the page: trees and balconies are ignored and roofs are flat. URLs carry the state: /warszawa/report/<id>, /warszawa/compare?ids=a,b,c, /warszawa/ranking.

Chapter 2

Reports read local copies of public data, so they take under a second

Asking a dozen public services for every report would be slow, and the report would break whenever one of them did. So the server downloads each source in bulk on a schedule and builds reports from its own copies.

All the sources are public: timetables, OpenStreetMap, the city's map server, and public registers for flood risk, air quality, schools, health care, addresses, buildings and flat sales. After the deploy of 15 November 2026, all 26 sources for Warsaw and all 20 for Kraków reported ready.

Each source refreshes on its own schedule, from daily to yearly. A typical report in a built-up area makes no outside request and takes 0.5 to 0.9 seconds, measured on 13 November on a 4-core server.

Some data needs cleaning first. The price data keeps only free-market flat sales, and air sensor readings more than four times off the city median are dropped.

A 121 MB data snapshot is committed to the repository, so a fresh copy starts with data. Some city portals block data-centre servers. So I refresh their data on my laptop and ship it to the server with one script.

How the local copies are stored and checked

City map layers are pulled in 4 to 8 km tiles. Geometry is stored as varint deltas, attribute sets are deduplicated and deflated, and everything is R-tree indexed in SQLite (better-sqlite3), one database per city. Image-only layers such as the 2 m noise map are reduced to one class byte per pixel. That is about 75 MB of layer data, and a full refresh cycle is roughly 1,000 requests a month. I checked the local noise copy against the live city map on 120 random points (720 lookups): 8 lookups (1.1%) differed by one 5 dB band, always where a band boundary passes within about 2 m. Public transport runs on my own timetable index and an earliest-arrival Connection Scan router over the GTFS feeds. The Warsaw seed holds 184,456 OpenStreetMap features, 127,028 address points and 153,109 buildings. Green cover adds tree crowns measured from two national elevation models: everything at least 2.5 m above the terrain that is rough like a crown and outside a building, on a 10 m grid (scripts/canopy-build.py). The seed export is deterministic and sorted line by line, so a data refresh shows up as a readable git diff.

Chapter 3

Walking times are measured along the streets

A shop 300 m away in a straight line can be a 2.5 km walk when a river or a railway is in the way. So the report measures every walk along the street network, and the minutes it shows are minutes a person would walk.

I built a walking network for each city from OpenStreetMap. From the address, one search over that network measures the way to stops, shops, doctors, schools, parks, car parks and city bikes. The nearest place of each kind gets its route drawn on the report map.

The network takes time to load after a restart, and some addresses are far from any mapped path. In those cases the app estimates the walk as the straight-line distance times 1.3 and marks it as an estimate. It switches to the network when it is ready. Counts such as "shops within 500 m" stay straight-line on purpose.

The same network also gives a step-free route, for a parent with a pram or an older person with a walking frame. Steps need a ramp, and rough paving and slopes cost more. The page says "step-free" and never "wheelchair accessible", because kerb heights and pavement widths are not mapped well enough to promise that.

One bug came from this network. When I added Kraków, its first walking network had been built at Warsaw's latitude. The network stores east-west distances scaled for one latitude, and Kraków lies about 2 degrees further south. So east-west walks in Kraków came out about 4.5% too short. Each city now builds its own network at its own latitude, and the checklist for adding a city says so.

How the walk search works

Walker (server/src/pois/walking.ts) runs one bounded Dijkstra search from the address, capped at 3,000 m (the furthest threshold that matters, the zero point for forests). It takes the 5 nearest places of each kind by straight line and re-ranks them by walking distance. A place not reached within the cap gets at least the cap, never a short straight-line guess. A route is stored when the walk is 2,000 m or less. Every section records its distanceBasis (network or line), and an old report without it is re-measured when opened. When places moved to network distances on 16 November, a backfill re-measured every stored report at start-up and kept its updatedAt, and the heat-map grid moved from version g7 to g8. Each risk in the plan had a named test, for example "walker: a target across a barrier gets the network distance" and "walker: unreached target gets at least the cap". The Warsaw graph is 19.3 MB, built from a Geofabrik extract with osmium. The Kraków rebuild at latitude 50.06 has 329,659 nodes (commit c48a33f). The commit measured the earlier error at about 4.5%, and docs/adding-a-city.md says up to 4.6%. Stops use the network too, with 8 and 12 minute catchments.

Chapter 4

Every score is simple arithmetic, and rules write the verdict

Each topic gets a score out of 100 from formulas simple enough to explain on the page. The verdict is written by rules, so the same report always gets the same words.

A distance counts in full up to a set distance, then falls in a straight line to zero. A count of places rises quickly and then levels off, so the tenth café matters less than the first. When a fact is unknown, it is left out and the other parts are reweighted. Missing data does not count as bad.

Kraków does not publish some things Warsaw does, such as school catchment areas and land use. It also has no public city bike system. For those, the report says "not published by the city". A line saying "none nearby" would be wrong, and a reader could not tell the two cases apart.

The verdict comes from rules in code, with thresholds taken from published norms such as the WHO noise guidelines and 400 m and 800 m walking catchments. It writes grammatical Polish, with the right gender, plural and case forms, as well as English. It has no randomness, and a test suite checks its output.

Visitors can raise or lower each topic with a slider. In the first version, moving one slider barely changed the order of places. The cause was that transport, shops, health, schools and leisure tend to rise and fall together, so together they outvoted any single topic. The four slider levels now multiply a topic's weight by 0, 1, 3 or 9. Set to the top level, quiet goes from about 11% of the overall score to about 53%.

The formulas and the verdict rules

In server/src/scoring.ts, distance parts are lin(v, full, zero), falling linearly from 1 at full metres to 0 at zero metres. Count parts saturate as 1 - exp(-count / k). weighted() drops parts whose value is null and renormalises the rest. Default weights (SCORE_WEIGHTS, shared/prefs.ts): transit 0.22, shopping 0.14, health 0.12, green 0.12, quiet 0.12, education 0.10, leisure 0.10, air 0.08, step-free 0.06. LEVEL_FACTOR = [0, 1, 3, 9] multiplies them (commit e326e24). The verdict (server/src/verdict.ts, 1,561 lines, with 1,463 lines of tests) is pure: no clock, no randomness, no I/O. It flattens the sections into facts, collects pros and cons with one polarity per topic, picks a headline, lists things to check in person, and scores six lifestyles (families, car-free, quiet seekers, city lovers, seniors, active people). Air thresholds are calibrated per city from the city's own station medians (npm run calibrate): PM2.5 18/25 µg/m³ for Kraków against 15/21 for Warsaw. cities/unpublished.ts lists the facts each city leaves out, and a saved report is recomputed when that list changes.

Chapter 5

AI agents wrote much of the code under written plans and tests

I used AI coding agents (Claude Code) for much of the implementation. I decided what to build, set the rules each change had to meet, and reviewed what came back. The agents worked fast, so the checks had to live in the repository, where every session would run into them.

For the first four days, the agents worked in cloud sessions and opened pull requests. I merged 10 of them. Git shows 124 of the 358 commits authored by Claude, all from that phase. From 14 November most commits were made by agents running on my laptop, and they committed under my name. So from that day git does not separate their commits from mine.

Each large change started as a written plan in the repository. A plan states the goal, the constraints that must hold, the commands that prove the work is done, and a short list of risks to review. Each risk names the test that guards it, for example "a shop across a river gets the walking distance". The plan also records my decisions, such as which sources' licence terms allow their use. The Kraków plan was reviewed by a separate model run before any code was written.

The checks run on every change. Tests cannot reach the internet, and a test that tries fails. The web build fails if a component contains text that is not translated, and a missing Polish phrase fails the type check. The same checks run before each commit and again on GitHub. Review rounds show up in git as their own commits, such as "Review fixes" and "Second review fixes".

The result was 358 commits in 8 days, about 50,000 lines of code and nearly 800 tests. Kraków went from a written plan to production in one day.

How the plans and checks were set up

Plans live in docs/superpowers/plans/ and open with "For agentic workers: REQUIRED SUB-SKILL: Use superpowers:subagent-driven-development", so an agent executes them task by task with checkbox steps, a failing test first, then the code, then the full suite. Each plan has "Global Constraints", "Verification commands" (npm test, npm run typecheck, npm --prefix web run build) and "Review Focus". The walking plan's review focus lists five risks, each tied to a named test. The Kraków plan says "Reviewed once by an independent Opus pass. Its findings are folded in." The onboarding plan was revised after a "cold review" (commit f70d814). Cloud-phase branches are named claude/.... Local commits were switched on with a marker file on 14 November (commit 6385b4d). 131 commits carry a co-author trailer naming Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.2 or Claude Sonnet 5.5. Guard rails: server/src/test-data-dir.ts refuses connections to outside hosts and fails the file that tried (commit 57fda8a). CI runs the tests behind a dead proxy at 127.0.0.1:9 so any network call fails fast. web/scripts/i18n-check.mjs fails the build on literal text in components. web/scripts/css-check.mjs stops page stylesheets from restyling the map. The web app has no test runner, so its pure logic lives in files with relative imports and is tested from the server runner (web/src/lib/compareCore.ts in server/src/web/compare-core.test.ts). Counts: 91 test files and 796 test cases by count on 17 November. The last full run recorded in the repository was 772 passing tests on 15 November. About 52,000 lines of non-test code (server 29,153, web 20,236, shared 3,057). CONTRIBUTING.md and docs/adding-a-city.md are written so that the next session, human or agent, starts from the current state.

Chapter 6

One small server runs a free public site with no accounts

The site is free, has no logins and runs on one rented server with 4 GB of memory. So the limits that keep it running are built into the server.

A point within 50 m of an existing report reopens that report in about 17 milliseconds. The server builds at most 4 reports at once and 500 new ones a day, and each visitor can create 10 a minute.

Personal settings stay in the visitor's own cookies, and the server never reads them. Visit statistics are self-hosted and never record an address, a search or coordinates.

A place stays in the ranking only while its data is at most 30 days old. A push to the main branch goes live within about 5 minutes plus the build.

Where it stands: Warsaw and Kraków are live, in Polish and English. Statistics started on 17 November 2026, so I have no traffic numbers yet. Wrocław is planned and not built. I have not tested the site with a screen reader or a browser's print dialog, or checked the rate limits behind the production proxy. The report is still too long on phones.

Limits, memory and load numbers

Rate limits per IP: create 10/min, refresh 10/min, travel times 60/min, report reads 120/min. Process-wide: REPORT_CONCURRENCY 4 with 50 waiting, REPORT_DAILY_CAP 500 new reports per UTC day across cities. Past the cap, creating answers 503 daily_cap with Retry-After, and the status page shows it. Reuse within 50 m does not count towards the cap. Each section has a 15 s timeout that does not count time spent waiting in the per-host HTTP queue. Load test on 13 November (4 vCPU): 10 concurrent creates finished in 9 s. 100 took 80 s, and 59 sections timed out. Flood and elevation have since moved to local files, so those sections no longer wait on outside services in most places. Memory with both cities loaded: 662 MB at rest, 840 to 900 MB during start-up, measured on 15 November. docker-compose.yml caps the container at 3 GB and the Node heap at 2,560 MB. The cookies are capped at 6,000 bytes together, below a common 8 KB header limit. Deploys: a cron job on the server fetches origin/main every 5 minutes and runs docker compose up -d --build when it moved. Ranking rules (shared/leaderboard.ts, pure functions): titles need 5 contenders city-wide and a score of 65, and contenders older than 25 days that someone opened in the last 90 days are refreshed in the background.

Six decisions and what each cost

Each row is a choice with an alternative I passed on. Read across for the trade-off, or open a row for the reasoning.

The questionWhat I choseWhat I passed onWhat it cost
  • Where does a report get its data? What I choseLocal copies of every source, downloaded in bulk on a schedule. What I passed onAsking each public service for every report. What it costAbout 75 MB of layer data per city, a refresh schedule per source, and a manual sync from my laptop.

    Asking per report would make each report as slow as the slowest portal, and one portal outage would break every report. Local copies make a typical report take under a second with no outside request. They also keep the load on the city's map server to roughly 1,000 requests a month.

    Evidence

    README "How it works"; commit 0a4ab36 ("Serve city facts, noise, flood extents and geocoding from local data"); scripts/sync-city.sh

  • How far away is the shop? What I choseWalking distance along the street network, with straight line times 1.3 only as a marked fallback. What I passed onStraight line times 1.3 for everything, which the first version used for shops. What it costA walking network of about 10 to 19 MB per city, built at each city's latitude, plus a re-measure of every stored report.

    A straight line ignores rivers, railways and motorways, and those are where the error is largest. Stops used the network from early on, and places followed on 16 November. Each section records which method it used, so an estimated distance is never shown as a measured one.

    Evidence

    server/src/pois/walking.ts; commits 680ab1d, 2667cfb, d02971e; docs/superpowers/plans/2026-11-16-walking-routes.md

  • Who writes the verdict? What I choseRules in code, with thresholds from published norms. What I passed onScores alone, leaving the reader to add them up. What it costAbout 1,500 lines of rules and as many lines of tests, and every new phrase written by hand in two languages.

    A score of 72 does not tell a reader whether the place is loud or far from a doctor. The verdict names the pros, the cons and what to check in person, in plain sentences, and every line comes from facts in the report. Because it is built from rules, the same report always gets the same verdict, and the rules are tested.

    Evidence

    server/src/verdict.ts (header: "Pure and deterministic"); server/src/verdict.test.ts

  • What does the report say when a city does not publish a fact? What I chose"Not published by the city", tracked per fact and per city. What I passed onTreating a missing source as "none nearby". What it costEvery section keeps a list of facts each city leaves out, and a saved report is recomputed when that list changes.

    "None nearby" would tell a Kraków reader there are no school catchments, when the city simply does not publish them. Unknown values are left out of the score, so missing data does not pull a place down.

    Evidence

    server/src/cities/unpublished.ts; docs/adding-a-city.md ("Say 'not published', not 'none nearby'")

  • How much should one priority slider matter? What I choseFour levels that multiply a topic's weight by 0, 1, 3 or 9. What I passed onA linear 0 to 3 scale. What it costAt the top level one topic carries about half of the overall score or more (quiet about 53%, transport about 70%), so a single slider can reorder the whole ranking.

    With a linear scale, one slider barely reordered places, because five related topics outvoted it. A steep scale makes a change visible on the map and in the ranking.

    Evidence

    shared/prefs.ts (LEVEL_FACTOR and its comment); web/src/lib/priorities.ts; commit e326e24

  • How do you keep a free site with no accounts from being overloaded? What I choseLimits in the server: reuse within 50 m, 4 reports at once, 500 new reports a day, 10 per minute per visitor. What I passed onAccounts and logins. What it costOn a very busy day, new reports stop until midnight UTC. Reports are public and permanent, and only I can delete one.

    Accounts would add friction for a visitor who wants one answer, and they would mean storing personal data. Server-side limits protect a 4 GB server without knowing who anyone is.

    Evidence

    CONTRIBUTING.md "Decisions worth knowing"; server/src/reports/queue.ts, server/src/reports/cap.ts

What I would change

Measure walks along the streets from day one. The first version estimated walks to places as a straight line times 1.3. Switching on 16 November meant re-measuring every stored report at start-up and recomputing the heat map for both cities. The scoring thresholds now read as walking metres, and they still need a review.

Decide what the first screen is for before building the ranking. People who got a link to the site thought it was a leaderboard, because the ranking panel opened first. On 16 November I closed the ranking by default and put a "Check your place" box on the first screen. That cost a redesign of the map's first screen and its menus.

Compute scores when a report is read, instead of storing them. Saved reports keep the scores from their last refresh. So a scoring change leaves old numbers on saved reports until I run a rescore command by hand. That cost a separate rescore tool and a manual step after every change. Recomputing on open is still on the open-work list.
